# Literature survey: climate (present and past) and the frequency, specialisation and type of biotic interactions

Theme: general theory and comparative evidence relevant to explaining the geography of four lycaenid larval strategies (NA = non-ant-associated; FM = facultative mutualist; OM = obligate mutualist; OP = obligate parasite) in 500-km cells.

Compiled 2026-10-28. Verification: every DOI below was resolved against the Crossref REST API (author list, year, title, journal, volume, pages). Abstracts were read from Crossref/OpenAlex/Europe PMC/Semantic Scholar, and full text where an open copy exists. "VERIFIED" means the metadata matched Crossref (or, for items without a DOI, two independent bibliographic sources). If only the citation could be checked and the content was not read, the entry says so.

Variable shorthand. Present: MAT (mean annual temperature), MAP (annual precipitation), AI (aridity index), TS/PS (temperature/precipitation seasonality), PV (inter-annual precipitation variability), NPP (productivity). Past: LGM-anom (LGM-to-present temperature/precipitation anomaly), VEL (Late-Quaternary climate-change velocity, as in Sandel et al. 2011), STAB (glacial-cycle stability, e.g. StableClim / CHELSA-TraCE21k variance), DEEP (time since Miocene–Pliocene aridification / Cenozoic biome stability). REALM = biogeographic realm or clade effect.

## A. Latitudinal gradients in the strength and specialisation of biotic interactions

**A1. Dobzhansky, T. (1950).** Evolution in the tropics. *American Scientist* 38: 209–221. No DOI (JSTOR stable 27826306 not checked; citation confirmed by the WKU reprint and the CSHL repository record). **VERIFIED (citation; no DOI)**
- Finding: this essay is the classic source of the argument that natural selection at high latitudes is driven mainly by the physical environment, whereas in the tropics it is driven by biotic interactions.
- Implication: ant association, as a biotic "solution", should be more common and more elaborate (OM, OP) in warm, aseasonal climates. NA should dominate cold, seasonal climates. Variables: MAT, TS, NPP.
- PDF: paywalled (JSTOR)

**A2. Schemske, D.W., Mittelbach, G.G., Cornell, H.V., Sobel, J.M. & Roy, K. (2009).** Is there a latitudinal gradient in the importance of biotic interactions? *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics* 40: 245–269. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ecolsys.39.110707.173430 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: the review mostly supports stronger interactions at low latitudes (herbivory, insect predation). It names ant–plant interactions as a predominantly tropical mutualism. Among studies that included the tropics, none found interactions more important at high latitudes.
- Implication: the proportion of ant-associated species (FM+OM+OP) should rise with MAT and NPP and fall with TS. This contradicts the observed dominance of FM/NA in the Neotropics and of obligate strategies in temperate and Mediterranean Australia and the Cape. Variables: MAT, TS, NPP.
- PDF: paywalled

**A3. Moles, A.T. & Ollerton, J. (2016).** Is the notion that species interactions are stronger and more specialized in the tropics a zombie idea? *Biotropica* 48: 141–145. https://doi.org/10.1111/btp.12281 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: a short critical review arguing that evidence for stronger *and more specialised* tropical interactions is weak and mixed. The idea persists despite contrary data.
- Implication: the null expectation is that specialisation (obligacy) need not rise toward the tropics. It supports testing climate effects other than latitude/temperature. Variables: latitude versus MAT, PS, STAB.
- PDF: moles_2016_zombie_idea.pdf

**A4. Roslin, T., Hardwick, B., Novotny, V. et al. (2017).** Higher predation risk for insect prey at low latitudes and elevations. *Science* 356: 742–744. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaj1631 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: across 31 sites spanning 11,660 km of latitude, attack rates on dummy caterpillars rose toward the equator and toward low elevations. The gradient was driven by arthropod predators (largely ants), not birds or mammals.
- Implication: predation pressure on caterpillars, partly from ants themselves, is highest in warm lowlands. That should raise the benefit of ant protection, favouring FM/OM. It also raises the cost of being ant-exposed without appeasement, which predicts fewer NA where ant pressure is high. Variables: MAT, elevation, NPP.
- PDF: roslin_2017_predation_latitude.pdf (author-accepted manuscript, Lancaster repository)

**A5. Hargreaves, A.L., Suárez, E., Mehltreter, K. et al. (2019).** Seed predation increases from the Arctic to the Equator and from high to low elevations. *Science Advances* 5: eaau4403. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aau4403 (erratum: 10.1126/sciadv.abn8360) **VERIFIED**
- Finding: 6,995 standardised seed depots on 18 mountains showed seed predation rising 17% from the Arctic to the Equator and 17% from 4,000 m to sea level. *Climate seasonality* explained the clines better than productivity, biodiversity or latitude.
- Implication: seasonality, not temperature or productivity per se, may be the best present-climate correlate of interaction intensity. Low TS predicts more ant-associated strategies; high TS predicts more NA. Variables: TS, PS.
- PDF: hargreaves_2019_seed_predation.pdf (bioRxiv preprint version 10.1101/304634)

**A6. Zvereva, E.L. & Kozlov, M.V. (2021).** Latitudinal gradient in the intensity of biotic interactions in terrestrial ecosystems: sources of variation and differences from the diversity gradient revealed by meta-analysis. *Ecology Letters* 24: 2506–2520. https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.13851 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: herbivory and carnivory decreased poleward, but *parasitism increased poleward*. The gradient was threefold stronger above 50–60° than at lower latitudes, was driven by ectothermic consumers, and was weaker than the diversity gradient.
- Implication: the direction may differ by interaction type. Higher-latitude enrichment of OP (e.g. Palaearctic *Phengaris*) is not inconsistent with general parasitism gradients. Expect a nonlinear (threshold) latitude/temperature effect. Variables: MAT (nonlinear), TS.
- PDF: paywalled (the Turku repository copy is behind bot protection)

**A7. Paquette, A. & Hargreaves, A.L. (2021).** Biotic interactions are more often important at species' warm versus cool range edges. *Ecology Letters* 24: 2427–2438. https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.13864 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: a review of 886 range limits found that biotic interactions (competition, predation, parasitism) limited warm edges more often than cool edges. Abiotic factors dominated cool edges, but temperature mattered at both.
- Implication: ant-dependent strategies (OM, OP) need ant partners whose own ranges are temperature-limited. The cool limits of OM/OP should track ant limits, so OM/OP proportions should fall at low MAT. Variables: MAT, minimum temperature.
- PDF: paquette_2021_range_edges_interactions.pdf (bioRxiv preprint)

**A8. Louthan, A.M., Doak, D.F. & Angert, A.L. (2015).** Where and when do species interactions set range limits? *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 30: 780–792. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2015.09.011 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: formalises Darwin's idea that abiotic forces set limits in stressful regions and species interactions set them in benign regions. The paper shows how interaction strength must vary along stress gradients to produce this pattern and finds the idea under-tested.
- Implication: provides a framework for predicting where ant dependence constrains lycaenid distributions (benign regions) versus where climate does. Variables: AI, MAT.
- PDF: paywalled

**A9. Vázquez, D.P. & Stevens, R.D. (2004).** The latitudinal gradient in niche breadth: concepts and evidence. *The American Naturalist* 164: E1–E19. https://doi.org/10.1086/421445 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: neither the assumptions nor the predictions of MacArthur's hypothesis (narrower niches in the tropics) were supported by data. The authors propose a non-equilibrium alternative based on interaction-network structure.
- Implication: there is no strong prior that obligate (narrow-niche) strategies should be tropical. Variables: latitude, MAT.
- PDF: paywalled

**A10. Ollerton, J. & Cranmer, L. (2002).** Latitudinal trends in plant–pollinator interactions: are tropical plants more specialised? *Oikos* 98: 340–350. https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0706.2002.980215.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: two independent datasets showed that plant–pollinator interactions are *not* more specialised in the tropics.
- Implication: the same as A9, a null expectation for the latitude–obligacy link. Variables: latitude.
- PDF: paywalled

**A11. Dyer, L.A., Singer, M.S., Lill, J.T. et al. (2007).** Host specificity of Lepidoptera in tropical and temperate forests. *Nature* 448: 696–699. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05884 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: at eight New World forest sites from 15°S to 55°N, tropical caterpillars fed on fewer plant species, genera and families than temperate ones.
- Implication: if specialisation in one dimension (host plant) correlates with specialisation in another (ant partner), OM/OP should be tropical. The Neotropical lycaenid pattern contradicts this, which points to a dimension-specific or clade-specific process. Variables: MAT, NPP.
- PDF: paywalled

**A12. Novotny, V., Drozd, P., Miller, S.E., Kulfan, M., Janda, M., Basset, Y. & Weiblen, G.D. (2006).** Why are there so many species of herbivorous insects in tropical rainforests? *Science* 313: 1115–1118. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1129237 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: host specificity of folivorous insects did not differ between temperate (Central Europe) and tropical (New Guinea) forests.
- Implication: contradicts A11; the latitude–specialisation link is not general. Variables: MAT.
- PDF: paywalled

**A13. Forister, M.L., Novotny, V., Panorska, A.K. et al. (2015).** The global distribution of diet breadth in insect herbivores. *PNAS* 112: 442–447. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1423042112 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: across more than 7,500 herbivore species, diet breadth followed a truncated Pareto distribution dominated by specialists. Diet breadth narrowed toward the tropics, and plant diversity contributed to, but did not fully explain, the gradient.
- Implication: weak global support for greater tropical specialisation of insect resource use; it predicts more OM/OP toward low latitudes, which the lycaenid data contradict. The contrast is itself a result worth stating. Variables: MAT, plant richness.
- PDF: forister_2015_diet_breadth_global.pdf (PMC)

**A14. Fiedler, K. (1998).** Diet breadth and host plant diversity of tropical- vs. temperate-zone herbivores: South-East Asian and West Palaearctic butterflies as a case study. *Ecological Entomology* 23: 285–297. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2311.1998.00132.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: there was no evidence that tropical butterflies are more specialised. In the Polyommatini (Lycaenidae), tropical species were *more* polyphagous. Phylogenetic conservatism and chemical constraints shape host use more than latitude.
- Implication: this is directly relevant to lycaenids. Clade identity (REALM/phylogeny) may outweigh climate. Lycaenid host-plant specialisation (which is linked to obligate ant association) is not tropical-biased. Variables: REALM, phylogenetic covariance.
- PDF: paywalled

**A15. Schleuning, M., Fründ, J., Klein, A.-M. et al. (2012).** Specialization of mutualistic interaction networks decreases toward tropical latitudes. *Current Biology* 22: 1925–1931. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.08.015 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: in a global set of pollination and seed-dispersal networks, specialisation was *lower* in the tropics. Specialisation was more closely related to *contemporary climate than to past climate stability*, and it decreased with local and regional plant diversity.
- Implication: this is the strongest counter-example to the climate-stability prediction (C4). Mutualism specialisation may track present climate and partner diversity. Higher OM/OP at mid-latitudes (Cape, southern Australia, Palaearctic) is consistent with it. Variables: MAT, MAP, plant/ant richness, VEL (expected weak).
- PDF: paywalled (Cell open-archive and Zurich repository copies were blocked by bot checks)

**A16. Dalsgaard, B., Schleuning, M., Maruyama, P.K. et al. (2017).** Opposed latitudinal patterns of network-derived and dietary specialization in avian plant–frugivore interaction systems. *Ecography* 40: 1395–1401. https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02604 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: network specialisation of frugivore networks *increased* with latitude. Yet the proportion of obligate frugivores was higher in the tropics, because obligate frugivores need diverse fruit resources and are network generalists.
- Implication: "obligate dependence on a resource type" and "partner specificity" can show opposite gradients. For lycaenids, obligacy (dependence on ants) and specificity (number of ant species) should be analysed separately. Variables: ant richness, MAT.
- PDF: paywalled

**A17. Trøjelsgaard, K. & Olesen, J.M. (2013).** Macroecology of pollination networks. *Global Ecology and Biogeography* 22: 149–162 (online 2012). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1466-8238.2012.00777.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: in 54 pollination networks, modularity decreased with latitude (more specialised toward the tropics), and links per plant and the animal/plant ratio peaked at mid-latitudes. *Mean annual precipitation* was linked to both nestedness and modularity.
- Implication: precipitation, more than temperature, may structure mutualism specialisation. Expect OM/OP proportions to vary with MAP. Variables: MAP, MAT.
- PDF: paywalled

**A18. Pellissier, L., Albouy, C., Bascompte, J. et al. (2018).** Comparing species interaction networks along environmental gradients. *Biological Reviews* 93: 785–800. https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.12366 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: a review of methods and evidence for network change along gradients. Interaction change comes from species turnover, abundance shifts and direct abiotic effects on interactions, and these components must be separated.
- Implication: methodological. Separate the effect of climate on *which clades* occur (composition) from its effect on *strategy within clades*, for example with within-clade or phylogenetically corrected models. Variables: all, plus REALM.
- PDF: paywalled (Wiley and HAL copies blocked by bot checks)

## B. Stress-gradient hypothesis, context dependence, and the mutualism–parasitism continuum

**B1. Bertness, M.D. & Callaway, R. (1994).** Positive interactions in communities. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 9: 191–193. https://doi.org/10.1016/0169-5347(94)90088-4 **VERIFIED** (abstract not available; content known from the literature)
- Finding: the founding statement of the stress-gradient hypothesis (SGH): positive interactions should be more frequent under high physical stress or consumer pressure.
- Implication: mutualistic ant association should be more frequent, and more obligate, in harsh (arid, nutrient-poor, hot) environments. This predicts more OM (and FM) with AI and lower NPP, and more NA in benign mesic climates. Variables: AI, MAP, NPP, soil fertility.
- PDF: paywalled

**B2. Callaway, R.M., Brooker, R.W., Choler, P. et al. (2002).** Positive interactions among alpine plants increase with stress. *Nature* 417: 844–848. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature00812 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: in experiments across 11 mountain ranges (115 species), competition dominated at lower, less stressful sites and facilitation at high, stressful sites. Positive interactions were more important where early-summer temperatures were low.
- Implication: a strong SGH test, but for plants and cold stress. For ant mutualisms, cold suppresses the ant partner, so the SGH may hold only along aridity/nutrient axes, not temperature. Variables: AI, not MAT.
- PDF: paywalled

**B3. He, Q., Bertness, M.D. & Altieri, A.H. (2013).** Global shifts towards positive species interactions with increasing environmental stress. *Ecology Letters* 16: 695–706. https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.12080 **VERIFIED** (note: the DOI is ele.12080, not ele.12079)
- Finding: a meta-analysis of 727 SGH tests in plants. Interactions shift to facilitation (survival) or reduced competition (growth) with stress, and never toward more competition. The result was consistent across stress types, climates and ecosystems.
- Implication: if lycaenid–ant mutualism behaves like facilitation, OM and FM should increase with aridity and low productivity, and NA should decrease. Variables: AI, NPP, PS.
- PDF: paywalled

**B4. Maestre, F.T., Callaway, R.M., Valladares, F. & Lortie, C.J. (2009).** Refining the stress-gradient hypothesis for competition and facilitation in plant communities. *Journal of Ecology* 97: 199–205. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2745.2008.01476.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: the outcome depends on the stress type (resource versus non-resource) and the life-history strategies of the interacting species. Facilitation can collapse at extreme stress.
- Implication: predicts a hump-shaped relationship. OM should peak at intermediate-to-high aridity (e.g. semi-arid Australia, the Karoo/fynbos margin) and decline in hyper-arid areas. Variables: AI (quadratic), MAP.
- PDF: paywalled (Wiley PDF blocked by bot check)

**B5. Holmgren, M. & Scheffer, M. (2010).** Strong facilitation in mild environments: the stress gradient hypothesis revisited. *Journal of Ecology* 98: 1269–1275. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2745.2010.01709.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: absolute facilitative effects are often largest under *moderate* stress. Facilitation can be overruled by competition for the same limiting resource in the harshest conditions.
- Implication: as for B4, a unimodal AI effect on OM/FM. Variables: AI (quadratic).
- PDF: paywalled

**B6. Bronstein, J.L. (1994).** Conditional outcomes in mutualistic interactions. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 9: 214–217. https://doi.org/10.1016/0169-5347(94)90246-1 **VERIFIED** (abstract not available; content known)
- Finding: mutualism outcomes vary with ecological context (partner abundance, third parties, resources) and can become neutral or antagonistic.
- Implication: provides a basis for a single continuum from FM to OM to OP, with climate shifting where species sit on it. Variables: NPP, AI, ant abundance.
- PDF: paywalled

**B7. Bronstein, J.L. (2009).** The evolution of facilitation and mutualism. *Journal of Ecology* 97: 1160–1170. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2745.2009.01566.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: links facilitation and mutualism theory across trait evolution, the specialisation–generalisation continuum, origins and maintenance, coevolution, and cheating.
- Implication: the conceptual link between the SGH (B1–B5) and lycaenid–ant mutualism. Cheating (parasitism) is an expected product of mutualism, so OP should co-occur where OM is common. Variables: (OP ~ OM correlation).
- PDF: paywalled

**B8. Chamberlain, S.A., Bronstein, J.L. & Rudgers, J.A. (2014).** How context dependent are species interactions? *Ecology Letters* 17: 881–890. https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.12279 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: a meta-analysis of 247 articles. Mutualism was the interaction most likely to change *sign* across contexts, although it did not strongly differ from competition. Variation was greatest along spatial and abiotic gradients.
- Implication: mutualism outcomes (FM vs OM vs OP) should be expected to shift along climatic gradients, supporting the study premise. Variables: all present-climate axes.
- PDF: paywalled

**B9. Hoeksema, J.D. & Bruna, E.M. (2000).** Pursuing the big questions about interspecific mutualism: a review of theoretical approaches. *Oecologia* 125: 321–330. https://doi.org/10.1007/s004420000496 **VERIFIED** (abstract not read)
**and Hoeksema, J.D. & Bruna, E.M. (2015).** Context-dependent outcomes of mutualistic interactions. In J.L. Bronstein (ed.) *Mutualism*, pp. 181–202. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199675654.003.0010 **VERIFIED** (chapter content not read)
- Finding (from the titles and known content): the 2000 paper is a review of mutualism theory, and the 2015 chapter synthesises how abiotic and biotic context alters mutualism outcomes.
- Implication: theoretical background for B6–B8. Variables: as above.
- PDF: paywalled

**B10. Thrall, P.H., Hochberg, M.E., Burdon, J.J. & Bever, J.D. (2007).** Coevolution of symbiotic mutualists and parasites in a community context. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 22: 120–126. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2006.11.007 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: predicts that symbiotic interactions become *less beneficial as environmental quality (productivity) increases*. As biotic complexity increases, pathogen specificity should fall and mutualist specificity should rise. The authors call for geographic-scale tests.
- Implication: a key prediction. OM should be most frequent in low-productivity (nutrient-poor, arid) regions. In productive regions, mutualism should weaken toward FM or tip toward OP. Variables: NPP, soil fertility, AI; OM:OP ratio versus NPP.
- PDF: paywalled

**B11. Johnson, N.C., Graham, J.H. & Smith, F.A. (1997).** Functioning of mycorrhizal associations along the mutualism–parasitism continuum. *New Phytologist* 135: 575–585. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.1997.00729.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: mycorrhizas can be parasitic when net costs exceed benefits. Parasitism can be developmentally, environmentally or genotypically induced, and abiotic and biotic factors mediate where an association sits on the continuum.
- Implication: an analogue for lycaenids. The same lineage can occupy mutualist or parasite positions depending on resource context. Supports modelling the OM and OP proportions jointly. Variables: NPP, nutrients.
- PDF: paywalled

**B12. Fowler, J.C., Donald, M.L., Bronstein, J.L. & Miller, T.E.X. (2023).** The geographic footprint of mutualism: how mutualists influence species' range limits. *Ecological Monographs* 93: e1558. https://doi.org/10.1002/ecm.1558 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: a literature synthesis. Mutualists can extend partner ranges through fitness benefits and dispersal, or constrain them where absent. Most footprints come from *changes in partner frequency from range core to edge*, not changes in interaction outcome.
- Implication: OM/OP distributions should be bounded by ant-partner distributions. Climate effects on lycaenid strategy may be mediated through climate effects on ant abundance. Test climate → ant richness/abundance → strategy (path or mediation model). Variables: ant richness (e.g. GABI), MAT, AI.
- PDF: paywalled (Wiley PDF blocked by bot check, although the article is open access)

**B13. Stephan, P., Bramon Mora, B. & Alexander, J.M. (2021).** Positive species interactions shape species' range limits. *Oikos* 130: 1611–1625. https://doi.org/10.1111/oik.08146 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: a review of 73 studies. *Obligate* interactions, especially obligate mutualisms, tend to constrain the ranges of one or both partners. *Facultative* positive interactions tend to widen ranges, with more context dependence.
- Implication: OM (and OP) should have smaller ranges and be filtered toward regions where partners are reliably present. FM should be widespread and less climate-structured. Variables: VEL, STAB, ant richness.
- PDF: paywalled (open-access article; Wiley PDF blocked)

**B14. Afkhami, M.E., McIntyre, P.J. & Strauss, S.Y. (2014).** Mutualist-mediated effects on species' range limits across large geographic scales. *Ecology Letters* 17: 1265–1273. https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.12332 **VERIFIED** (note: the DOI is ele.12332, not ele.12327)
- Finding: fungal endophytes relieved drought stress and extended the grass *Bromus laevipes* about 20% further into drier habitat.
- Implication: an empirical case of the SGH at range scale. A mutualist lets its host occupy arid areas, so ant association might let lycaenids persist in arid regions, raising OM/FM with AI. Variables: AI, MAP.
- PDF: paywalled

## C. Climate stability, Quaternary climate-change velocity and specialisation

**C1. Dynesius, M. & Jansson, R. (2000).** Evolutionary consequences of changes in species' geographical distributions driven by Milankovitch climate oscillations. *PNAS* 97: 9115–9120. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.97.16.9115 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: proposes "orbitally forced range dynamics" (ORD), which select *against specialisation*, favour dispersability, and increase range size and polyploidy. ORD increases with latitude.
- Implication: the core palaeoclimate hypothesis. OM and OP (dual dependence on host plant and ant) should be rarer where Quaternary ORD was large; FM and NA should be more common there. Variables: VEL, LGM-anom, STAB.
- PDF: dynesius_2000_milankovitch_range_dynamics.pdf (PMC)

**C2. Jansson, R. & Dynesius, M. (2002).** The fate of clades in a world of recurrent climatic change: Milankovitch oscillations and evolution. *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics* 33: 741–777. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ecolsys.33.010802.150520 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: high ORD should produce clades with large ranges, high vagility, *low specialisation* and little geographic subdivision. Low-ORD areas accumulate persistent, divergent lineages.
- Implication: as for C1, with explicit global predictions. Expect OM/OP enrichment in low-ORD regions such as the Cape and SW Australia. Variables: VEL, LGM-anom.
- PDF: paywalled

**C3. Jansson, R. (2003).** Global patterns in endemism explained by past climatic change. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 270: 583–590. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2002.2283 **VERIFIED** (note: the DOI ends 2283, not 2282)
- Finding: the larger the mean-annual-temperature change since the LGM, the fewer endemic mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and plants. This held regardless of area, latitude, former glaciation or insularity.
- Implication: provides the LGM temperature anomaly as a simple predictor. Predicts more OM/OP where LGM-anom is small. Variables: LGM-anom (temperature).
- PDF: jansson_2003_endemism_past_climate.pdf (PMC)

**C4. Dalsgaard, B., Magård, E., Fjeldså, J. et al. (2011).** Specialization in plant–hummingbird networks is associated with species richness, contemporary precipitation and Quaternary climate-change velocity. *PLoS ONE* 6: e25891. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0025891 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: in 31 networks across the Americas, specialisation was higher at low latitudes (latitude explained 20–22%). Species richness, contemporary *precipitation* and Quaternary *velocity* together explained 53–64%, with higher specialisation where velocity was low. Temperature and seasonality were unimportant.
- Implication: the closest methodological model. Regress OM and OP proportions on MAP, VEL and richness. Predict OM/OP to rise with MAP (in their system) and fall with VEL. Variables: MAP, VEL, richness (lycaenid or ant).
- PDF: dalsgaard_2011_hummingbird_velocity.pdf (other agent)

**C5. Dalsgaard, B., Trøjelsgaard, K., Martín González, A.M. et al. (2013).** Historical climate-change influences modularity and nestedness of pollination networks. *Ecography* 36: 1331–1340. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0587.2013.00201.x **VERIFIED**
- Finding: on mainlands, Quaternary climate change was negatively associated with modularity, and nestedness was highest where Quaternary change was high. Historical climate was at least as important as contemporary climate. Effects were weak on islands.
- Implication: high VEL is expected to favour generalised, nested structures (FM, NA) and low VEL modular specialised ones (OM, OP). Variables: VEL, LGM-anom.
- PDF: paywalled

**C6. Sonne, J., Martín González, A.M., Maruyama, P.K. et al. (2016).** High proportion of smaller ranged hummingbird species coincides with ecological specialization across the Americas. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 283: 20152512. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2015.2512 **VERIFIED**
- Finding: in 46 networks, the proportion of small-ranged species was positively related to community specialisation, even after accounting for climate. The effect of small-ranged species on specialisation was much stronger than the reverse, suggesting that *climate acts on specialisation mainly through range-size dynamics*.
- Implication: predicts that the OM/OP share co-varies with the share of small-ranged lycaenids. Including range size (or endemism) as a mediator could separate a direct climate effect from a range-dynamics effect. Variables: VEL/STAB → range size → OM/OP.
- PDF: sonne_2016_hummingbird_range_specialisation.pdf (PMC)

**C7. Zanata, T.B., Dalsgaard, B., Passos, F.C. et al. (2017).** Global patterns of interaction specialization in bird–flower networks. *Journal of Biogeography* 44: 1891–1910. https://doi.org/10.1111/jbi.13045 **VERIFIED**

## Hypotheses from this literature

Notation: ↑ = proportion predicted to increase along the stated gradient, ↓ = decrease, ∩ = hump-shaped, 0 = no clear prediction. The four strategies are NA / FM / OM / OP.

**H1. Latitudinal biotic-interaction (ant-pressure) hypothesis** (A1, A2, A4, A5, G2–G4). Interactions, including ant abundance, activity and predation, are stronger in warm, aseasonal, productive climates. Ant protection matters more there, and being ant-naïve costs more.
- Gradient: higher MAT and NPP, lower TS.
- Predictions: NA ↓, FM ↑, OM ↑ (weakly), OP 0/↑.
- Status: largely contradicted by the observed Neotropics (FM/NA dominate) and the mid-latitude obligate hotspots. It probably explains FM versus NA better than obligacy.

**H2. Stress-gradient / harsh-environment mutualism hypothesis** (B1–B5, B14). Mutualism, and dependence on it, rises with abiotic stress (aridity, nutrient poverty), possibly peaking at intermediate-high stress.
- Gradient: higher AI (aridity) and lower NPP (quadratic terms advised).
- Predictions: NA ↓, FM ∩, OM ↑ or ∩ (peak in semi-arid), OP ↑ (follows OM; parasitism arises from mutualism).
- Status: consistent with Australia and the Karoo/Cape.

**H3. Productivity–benefit (mutualism–parasitism continuum) hypothesis** (B10, B11). Interactions become less beneficial as environmental quality rises. Obligate mutualism should be favoured in poor environments, with drift toward facultative use or exploitation in rich ones.
- Gradient: higher NPP and soil fertility.
- Predictions: NA ↑, FM ↑, OM ↓, OP ↑ relative to OM (the OM:OP ratio falls with NPP).

**H4. Quaternary stability / orbitally forced range dynamics hypothesis** (C1–C10, C15, C16). Large glacial–interglacial range dynamics select against specialisation and dual dependence. Specialised strategies persist in low-velocity refugia.
- Gradient: higher VEL, LGM-anom and instability (lower STAB).
- Predictions: NA ↑, FM ↑, OM ↓, OP ↓ (strongest).
- Status: supports the Cape and SW Australia; tension with Palaearctic OP (see Contradictions). Expected effect size is small if Schleuning et al. (A15) generalise.

**H5. Partner-tracking / dependence-filter hypothesis** (B12, B13, E1–E2, E6, E9). Obligate strategies need co-occurring host plants and specific ants and so lag behind climate and are filtered from recently recolonised, isolated or high-velocity areas.
- Gradient: higher VEL, insularity, and distance to LGM refugia.
- Predictions: sensitivity ordered OP > OM > FM > NA. NA ↑, FM ↑/0, OM ↓, OP ↓↓.
- Distinguish from H4 by testing whether the effect operates through ant-partner layers (mediation) and range size (C6).

**H6. OCBIL / old-infertile-landscape hypothesis** (C18, G7, G8, D4). Old, climatically buffered, nutrient-poor landscapes favour persistence and specialised biotic interactions, including convergent ant mutualisms (myrmecochory) in SW Australia and the Cape.
- Gradient: lower VEL and lower soil P/fertility, with a Mediterranean-type climate (high PS, winter rain).
- Predictions: NA ↓, FM 0/↓, OM ↑, OP ↑.

**H7. Environmental-uncertainty / bet-hedging hypothesis** (F1, F2, F5). Temporally variable climates favour cooperative or insurance strategies and generalised host use by parasites.
- Gradient: higher PV and PS.
- Predictions: NA ↓, FM ↑, OM ↑. For OP, the proportion is uncertain (0), but host breadth should increase with PV.
- Alternative (F3): obligate association may have *evolved* in benign conditions and *enabled colonisation* of variable or harsh ones. A cell-level correlation would look the same, so phylogenetic transition-order tests are needed.

**H8. Ant-partner-pool (mediation) hypothesis** (B12, D6–D8, G2, G4, E4, E6). Climate acts mainly through the richness, abundance, dominance and colony traits of ants: warmth favours tending ants (FM/OM), and cool-temperate polygynous host genera (*Myrmica*, *Formica*) favour social parasites (OP).
- Path: climate → ant richness, abundance, dominant-genus share or host genera → strategy.
- Predictions:
  - Along ant richness/abundance: NA ↓, FM ↑, OM ↑, OP 0.
  - Along *Myrmica*/*Formica* share: OP ↑.
- Southern-hemisphere ant richness (D7) and arid Australian ant megadiversity (D8) predict more OM where ant diversity is high at a given climate.

**H9. Deep-time aridification / Neogene legacy hypothesis** (D3–D5, C19, D7). Obligate ant association radiated with Miocene–Pliocene aridification and sclerophyll/arid biome expansion in Australia and southern Africa. Northern Cenozoic cooling caused extinction of ant-dependent lineages.
- Gradient: older aridification (DEEP), Southern Hemisphere.
- Predictions: NA ↓, FM 0, OM ↑, OP ↑ (Cape).
- Tested with realm and palaeo-biome terms. Present climate should explain little once these are included.

**H10. Phylogenetic-legacy / niche-conservatism null hypothesis** (D1, D2, D9, A14, C7). Strategy proportions reflect clade composition, since highly ant-associated lineages dominate the Southern Hemisphere fauna. Climate correlations arise only because clades are climatically conserved.
- Gradient: REALM or clade composition.
- Prediction: within-clade (or phylogenetically corrected) climate effects ≈ 0 for all four strategies.
- This is the essential null to report alongside climate models, via variance partitioning of climate versus REALM versus spatial terms.

**H11. Host-plant nitrogen / N-fixer hypothesis** (G9, G10, D9, G5). Ant association, which requires nutrient-rich larval secretions, is linked to protein-rich hosts (legumes, N-fixers, EFN-bearing Fabaceae). These are relatively most common in warm, dry, alkaline-soil biomes.
- Gradient: higher AI and MAT, and a higher N-fixer share.
- Predictions: NA ↓, FM ↑, OM ↑, OP 0.
- This is a mediation hypothesis requiring host-plant data. It is cited from Pierce et al. (2002) and should be checked against the primary lycaenid literature before use.

**H12. Parasitism gradient hypothesis** (A6, F7, F10). Parasitism, including social parasitism of ants, increases toward higher latitudes and cooler, more seasonal climates.
- Gradient: lower MAT and higher TS.
- Predictions: OP ↑, and the OP share of all ant-associated species ↑. NA ↑, FM ↓, OM ↓.
- Status: fits the Palaearctic OP concentration. Based on general parasite meta-analysis and ant social-parasite biogeography, not on lycaenid data.

## Contradictions and gaps

1. **The direction of the latitude–specialisation gradient is disputed.**
   - Evidence for greater tropical specialisation: Dyer et al. 2007 and Forister et al. 2015 (herbivore diet breadth), Dalsgaard et al. 2011 (hummingbird networks).
   - Evidence against: Novotny et al. 2006 and Fiedler 1998 (no difference, or more polyphagous tropical Polyommatini), and Schleuning et al. 2012 and Dalsgaard et al. 2017 (mutualistic networks *less* specialised in the tropics).
   - Moles & Ollerton (2016) and Vázquez & Stevens (2004) argue that the general expectation is unsupported. The lycaenid pattern (obligate strategies at mid-latitudes and in the Southern Hemisphere) fits the "less tropical specialisation" camp.
2. **Present versus past climate.**
   - Past climate stronger: Dalsgaard et al. 2011/2013 and Sonne et al. 2016 found Quaternary velocity important.
   - Present climate stronger: Schleuning et al. 2012, with a larger, global, mixed-mutualism dataset, found contemporary climate more important than past stability.
   - Expect modest unique variance for velocity once present climate and realm are included. Shared (confounded) variance between VEL and present climate will be large.
3. **Palaearctic obligate parasites versus the stability hypothesis.** *Phengaris* and other Palaearctic OP occur in a high-velocity, glaciated region, contrary to H4/H5.
   - Possible reconciliations: (a) OP occupy southern refugial or steppe areas (Hewitt; Araújo et al.); (b) parasitism shows a poleward gradient generally (Zvereva & Kozlov 2021) and social parasites of ants are Holarctic-biased (Buschinger 2009, content to verify); (c) host switching buffers OP against climate (Filz & Schmitt 2015).
   - The VEL effect may therefore differ in sign between OM and OP. Model them separately or as a multinomial.
4. **The sign of Quaternary effects differs between realms.** Northern glaciation caused extinction (Dunn et al. 2009; Hewitt). Australian glacial aridity may have *promoted* ant speciation (Andersen 2016; Byrne et al. 2008, multiple refugia). This suggests a VEL × REALM interaction rather than a global slope.
5. **Correlation versus causal order.** Cornwallis et al. (2017) show that strategy–environment correlations can arise because a strategy enables colonisation of harsh environments, not because harshness selects for it. Cell-level regressions cannot separate the two. Phylogenetic transition analyses (ancestral climatic niches of lineages switching to OM/OP) are needed. The study design should avoid causal language.
6. **Stress-gradient evidence is plant-centred.** Almost all SGH tests (He et al. 2013; Callaway et al. 2002) concern plant–plant facilitation. No macroecological SGH test exists for insect–ant mutualisms. Cold stress cannot raise ant mutualism the way it raises plant facilitation, because cold suppresses ants (Rico-Gray et al. 1998; Keeler 1979). The relevant stress axis for ants is aridity or nutrients, not cold.
7. **No study has explained *proportions of interaction strategies* of an animal clade with climate at global scale.**
   - Closest analogues: Steidinger et al. 2019 (mycorrhizal types of trees), Tamme et al. 2021 (N-fixers), Delavaux et al. 2019 (mycorrhizal filter), Jetz & Rubenstein 2011 (cooperative breeding), Lengyel et al. 2010 (myrmecochory hotspots; descriptive only, no climate model).
   - No study links Quaternary velocity to facultative versus obligate mutualism or to mutualism versus parasitism. This is a clear gap the new study fills.
8. **Partner data gap.** Several hypotheses (H5, H8, H11) need ant-partner or host-plant layers. These were not verified in this survey (ant databases such as GABI are being handled by another agent; a guenard_2017_gabi.pdf is in the folder). Without them, climate effects mediated by partners cannot be separated from direct effects.
9. **Brief corrections** (also flagged in the entries):
   - Schweiger et al. 2008 is butterfly–host-plant mismatch, not plant–pollinator.
   - Pigot & Tobias 2013 is about competition and secondary sympatry, not partner co-migration.
   - Medina & Langmore 2016 does not address climate; the climate paper is Antonson et al. 2020.
   - Cornwallis et al. 2017 reverses the assumed causality.
   - Hargreaves et al. 2019 is in *Science Advances*.
   - Correct DOIs: He et al. 2013 = 10.1111/ele.12080; Afkhami et al. 2014 = 10.1111/ele.12332; Jansson 2003 = 10.1098/rspb.2002.2283.
   - Trøjelsgaard & Olesen and Weber & Keeler were published online the year before their issue year.
10. **Unverified or partly verified content.**
    - Buschinger 2009: the content claim (Holarctic bias of ant social parasites) is from secondary knowledge.
    - Hopper 2009, Milewski & Bond 1982, Rico-Gray & Oliveira 2007, Hoeksema & Bruna 2000/2015, Gilman et al. 2010, Bertness & Callaway 1994, Bronstein 1994, Wiens & Donoghue 2004, Hewitt 2000: citations were verified, but abstracts were unavailable or full texts not read. Findings are stated from standard knowledge and should be checked before quoting specifics.
    - Article numbers for Antonson et al. 2020 (4185), Economo et al. 2018 (1778) and StableClim (335) are now confirmed in Crossref.
