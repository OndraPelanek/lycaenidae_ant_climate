**How the entries were checked.** "VERIFIED" means the authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages and DOI were matched against the Crossref record for that DOI. In that case the bibliographic details were confirmed from the item itself, from AntCat or BHL, or from at least two independent citing sources. "UNVERIFIED" means the reference could not be confirmed in any primary index. Where the survey brief framed a paper differently from what the paper reports, the entry has a **Correction** note.

## A. Global syntheses of lycaenid–ant associations and their geography

1. **Pierce NE (1987).** The evolution and biogeography of associations between lycaenid butterflies and ants. *Oxford Surveys in Evolutionary Biology* 4: 89–116. No DOI.
   - Status: VERIFIED (no DOI). The citation is confirmed by Pierce & Dankowicz 2022, the Pierce lab publication list and Espeland et al. 2023. The original was not read; no legal open-access copy was found, and the Pierce lab site blocks scripted access.
   - Finding: Pierce & Dankowicz (2022) summarise the paper's central observation. Obligate lycaenid–ant interactions are considerably more common in the Southern Hemisphere, particularly in Australia and southern Africa, than in the Nearctic and Palaearctic.
   - Correction: the brief's list of hypotheses in this paper (ant dominance, nutrient-poor soils, nitrogen-rich hosts) could not be checked against the original. Pierce's nitrogen hypothesis is published separately as Pierce 1985.
   - Supports: more obligate associations in Southern-Hemisphere Australia and southern Africa. This is the baseline pattern the new study models.
   - PDF: paywalled / not available.

2. **Pierce NE, Braby MF, Heath A, Lohman DJ, Mathew J, Rand DB, Travassos MA (2002).** The ecology and evolution of ant association in the Lycaenidae (Lepidoptera). *Annual Review of Entomology* 47: 733–771. doi:10.1146/annurev.ento.47.091201.145257
   - Status: VERIFIED (PubMed 11729090).
   - Finding: most of the roughly 6,000 lycaenids associate with ants, facultatively or obligately, along a continuum from mutualism to parasitism. Parasitism typically arises from mutualism. The abstract states that obligate associations are more common in the Southern Hemisphere "in part because highly ant-associated lineages make up a larger proportion of the fauna in these regions". The Neotropical fauna is flagged as poorly known.
   - Supports: lineage history (phylogenetic composition) as at least a partial explanation of the hemispheric pattern. This competes with purely climatic explanations.
   - PDF: paywalled.

3. **Pierce NE, Dankowicz E (2022).** The natural history of caterpillar-ant associations. In Marquis RJ, Koptur S (eds) *Caterpillars in the Middle*. Fascinating Life Sciences, Springer, pp. 319–391. doi:10.1007/978-3-030-86688-4_11
   - Status: VERIFIED. Full text read.
   - Finding: at least 3,841 (71%) of the ~5,390 lycaenids and 308 (20%) of the ~1,562 riodinids are known or inferred to be ant-associated. Highly specialised and obligate associations are concentrated in the Old World tropics, Australia and southern Africa. The chapter's section "Abiotic effects, obligate associations, and biogeography" sets out several non-exclusive explanations:
     - (i) climate;
     - (ii) soil micronutrients, especially phosphorus- and sodium-poor soils, which make ant-attracting secretions more valuable;
     - (iii) Miocene aridification, with larvae entering nests to escape drought and fire;
     - (iv) the absence of cyclical glaciation in the Southern Hemisphere, which leaves old, weathered soils;
     - (v) temperate seasonal fluctuation disrupting ant territoriality and partner reliability, and lengthening larval development.

     They note that temperate obligate associates spend most of their life inside ant nests. Only non-myrmecophilous or facultative lineages have spread widely beyond the southern regions; strongly ant-associated lineages apparently failed to cross Beringia. They also flag high ant association in the Brazilian Cerrado.
   - Supports: every major hypothesis below. This is the single best source for predictions.
   - PDF: **pierce_2022_caterpillar_ant_natural_history.pdf**

4. **Fiedler K (1991).** Systematic, evolutionary, and ecological implications of myrmecophily within the Lycaenidae (Insecta: Lepidoptera: Papilionoidea). *Bonner Zoologische Monographien* 31: 1–210.
   - Status: VERIFIED (no DOI). Confirmed from the ZOBODAT catalogue, ISBN 9783925382338 and multiple citing papers. Not read.
   - Finding: the foundational comparative monograph. It codes myrmecophily across the Lycaenidae and includes behavioural assays of 7 European lycaenids and 1 riodinid. Espeland et al. 2023 cite it, with Cottrell 1984 and Fiedler 1998, for the idea that harsh above-ground conditions (fire, dry seasons) triggered shifts into ant nests.
   - Supports: the harsh-surface/refuge hypothesis (H4), and the source of the trait coding.
   - PDF: not available.

5. **Fiedler K (1995).** Lycaenid butterflies and plants: is myrmecophily associated with particular hostplant preferences? *Ethology Ecology & Evolution* 7: 107–132. doi:10.1080/08927014.1995.9522960
   - Status: VERIFIED.
   - Finding: life-history data for 1,050 phytophagous Lycaeninae show that myrmecophily is more common among legume feeders than non-legume feeders at the subfamily level.
   - Correction: this is a correlation largely carried by particular clades. Pierce & Dankowicz (2022) state that "the relationship may not be causal".
   - Supports: a weak version of the nitrogen-rich host hypothesis (H8), confounded by phylogeny.
   - PDF: paywalled.

6. **Fiedler K (1996).** Host-plant relationships of lycaenid butterflies: large-scale patterns, interactions with plant chemistry, and mutualism with ants. In *Proceedings of the 9th International Symposium on Insect-Plant Relationships* (Series Entomologica), pp. 259–267. doi:10.1007/978-94-009-1720-0_59. Also published in *Entomologia Experimentalis et Applicata* 80: 259–267.
   - Status: VERIFIED (Crossref chapter record). No abstract available.
   - Finding: reviews large-scale patterns in lycaenid host-plant use and their links to myrmecophily. These include over-representation of legume and other nitrogen-rich hosts among myrmecophiles; this is inferred from the title and from Fiedler 1995.
   - Supports: H8.
   - PDF: paywalled.

7. **Fiedler K (1998a).** Lycaenid-ant interactions of the *Maculinea* type: tracing their historical roots in a comparative framework. *Journal of Insect Conservation* 2: 3–14. doi:10.1023/A:1009618721239
   - Status: VERIFIED (abstract summary only).
   - Finding: comparative evidence suggests that *Maculinea* evolved from facultative, unspecific ant mutualists, with Lamiaceae as ancestral hosts. It probably originated in eastern central Asia.
   - Supports: Palaearctic obligate parasites as a derived offshoot of facultative mutualists (lineage history); parasitism evolves from mutualism.
   - PDF: paywalled.

8. **Fiedler K (1998b).** Geographical patterns in life-history traits of Lycaenidae butterflies – ecological and evolutionary implications. *Zoology (Analysis of Complex Systems)* 100: 336–347.
   - Status: **UNVERIFIED**. The citation comes only from Pierce & Dankowicz 2022; there is no Crossref or index record, and the year may be 1997.
   - Finding: Pierce & Dankowicz cite it for the contrast between low obligate association in the temperate Western Palaearctic and high obligate association in temperate Australia.
   - Supports: the claim that climate alone cannot explain the pattern, since comparable temperate climates show different association levels (H1/H5/H6).
   - PDF: not found.

9. **Fiedler K (2001).** Ants that associate with Lycaeninae butterfly larvae: diversity, ecology and biogeography. *Diversity and Distributions* 7: 45–60. doi:10.1046/j.1472-4642.2001.00096.x
   - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
   - Finding:
     - Among 435 Lycaeninae, at least 53 ant genera from 6 subfamilies attend larvae. All of them also collect other liquid carbohydrates. *Crematogaster* and *Camponotus* are by far the most important partners.
     - Rarefied association diversity was highest in the Oriental, Australian and Nearctic regions, lower in Africa and the Palaearctic. The Neotropics could not be assessed.
     - The number of facultative lycaenid associates per ant genus is predicted by the genus's ecological prevalence and geographic distribution, not by its species richness.
     - Obligate interactions almost exclusively involve ecologically dominant ants with long-lived colonies.
   - Supports: ant availability and prevalence (H7) for facultative species, and ant dominance and colony longevity (H2) for obligate mutualists and parasites.
   - PDF: paywalled.

10. **Fiedler K (2006).** Ant-associates of Palaearctic lycaenid butterfly larvae (Hymenoptera: Formicidae; Lepidoptera: Lycaenidae) – a review. *Myrmecologische Nachrichten* 9: 77–87.
    - Status: VERIFIED (no DOI). Confirmed through citations in Pierce & Dankowicz 2022 and AntCat. Not read.
    - Finding: as cited by Pierce & Dankowicz 2022, temperate obligate associates generally spend most of their life cycle inside ant nests, where food, favourable microclimates and ants are always available.
    - Supports: the cold-climate buffering by ant nests that lets nest-dwelling parasites such as *Phengaris* persist where free-living obligate mutualists cannot (H3).
    - PDF: not downloaded.

11. **Fiedler K (2012).** The host genera of ant-parasitic Lycaenidae butterflies: a review. *Psyche* 2012: 153975. doi:10.1155/2012/153975
    - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
    - Finding:
      - *Camponotus*, *Crematogaster*, *Myrmica* and *Oecophylla* are the most often parasitised genera. Only *Camponotus* and *Crematogaster* have been invaded by several independent lineages.
      - The best single predictor of parasitism in an ant genus is the number of non-parasitic lycaenids associated with it. Neither its richness nor its ecological prevalence or distribution predicts parasitism.
      - Dominant *Formica* and *Dolichoderus* host no parasites; *Lasius* and *Polyrhachis* host very few.
    - Supports: parasites arise where mutualist associations are already dense (H7). Parasite share should track mutualist share and the ranges of *Camponotus* and *Crematogaster*.
    - PDF: OA, download blocked (Hindawi/Wiley).

12. **Fiedler K (2021).** The ant associates of Lycaenidae butterfly caterpillars – revisited. *Nota Lepidopterologica* 44: 159–174. doi:10.3897/nl.44.68993
    - Status: VERIFIED. Full text read.
    - Finding:
      - Covers 523 species and 63 ant genera. Over 98% of records involve nectarivorous or trophobiotic Formicinae, Myrmicinae or Dolichoderinae; *Crematogaster* and *Camponotus* top the list.
      - Dominant, large, long-lived colonies are over-represented as hosts of obligate species.
      - Association diversity is highest in the Oriental and especially the Australian region, and lowest in the Neotropical and Afrotropical regions.
      - In Africa the low diversity reflects two Neogene radiations: *Lepidochrysops*, which stays with *Camponotus*, and Aphnaeinae, which stay with *Crematogaster*.
      - For the Neotropics he offers four reasons: poorly documented life histories; riodinids occupying the niche; one dominant clade (Eumaeini); and leafcutter and army ants dominating ant assemblages while never tending trophobionts.
      - Holarctic *Lasius* and *Formica* are over-represented in proportion to their ecological prevalence in northern woodland and grassland.
    - Correction: low *partner diversity* in Africa does not mean low *obligate incidence*.
    - Supports: H6 (lineage history), H7 (ant availability), and Neotropical ant-community composition as a negative driver.
    - PDF: **fiedler_2021_ant_associates_revisited.pdf**

## B. Regional faunas, climate gradients and attendance

13. **Eastwood R, Fraser AM (1999).** Associations between lycaenid butterflies and ants in Australia. *Australian Journal of Ecology* (now *Austral Ecology*) 24: 503–537. doi:10.1046/j.1440-169x.1999.01000.x
    - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
    - Finding:
      - The review documents 265 associations and 65 non-attendance records.
      - Nearly 80% of Australian lycaenids with known early stages are ant-associated, and half of these are obligate.
      - Dolichoderinae, and to some extent Formicinae, are over-represented. All tending ants forage on vegetation for plant or insect nectar.
      - There is a "robust relationship between the competitive status of ants within a community, and their frequency and degree of association". Obligate species are highly partner-specific; the exceptions are *Ogyris aenone* and *O. amaryllis*.
      - Facultative species associate with dominant ants *less* often than dominant-ant abundance would predict.
    - Supports: H2 (ant dominance and competitive status favour obligacy) and H7. Also raises a contradiction: dominant ants are not universally good partners.
    - PDF: paywalled.

14. **Heath A, Claassens AJM (2003).** Ant-association among southern African Lycaenidae. *Journal of the Lepidopterists' Society* 57(1): 1–16. No DOI.
    - Status: VERIFIED (no DOI). Authors, title, volume and pages confirmed from the PDF itself.
    - Finding:
      - Of 392 southern African lycaenids, over three-quarters are ant-associated, and two-thirds of those are obligate.
      - Aphnaeini make up one-third of all lycaenids and are almost all obligate. Polyommatini make up another third, are 95% ant-associated, and split roughly evenly between facultative and obligate. Theclini are facultative; Poritiinae are not associated.
      - *Crematogaster* (Myrmicinae) and *Anoplolepis* (Formicinae) account for almost 80% of obligate relationships. *Thestor* larvae live under boulders over nests.
    - Supports: the southern-African obligate excess (baseline) and partner identity for H7.
    - PDF: **heath_2003_southern_african_ant_association.pdf**

15. **Pellissier L, Litsios G, Fiedler K, Pottier J, Dubuis A, Pradervand J-N, Salamin N, Guisan A (2012).** Loss of interactions with ants under cold climate in a regional myrmecophilous butterfly fauna. *Journal of Biogeography* 39: 1782–1790. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2699.2012.02743.x
    - Status: VERIFIED. Structured abstract read through the UNIL IRIS record.
    - Finding:
      - The study is set in the western Swiss Alps. Low-temperature tolerance was estimated from stratified random field sampling plus climate maps.
      - Ancestral-state reconstruction showed strong myrmecophily with secondary losses. Twenty-nine lycaenid species were recorded, but analyses used only the 24 Polyommatinae.
      - Species with stronger ant interactions occurred, on average, at warmer sites. Species in the coldest environments showed little or no ant interaction.
      - The authors also tested whether community-mean myrmecophily rises with temperature.
      - Conclusion: cold climates at high elevation filter out highly myrmecophilous species and may have been the evolutionary force behind loss of the mutualism. Holarctic-wide sampling is needed to separate ecological from evolutionary effects.
    - Correction: this is a regional study of one subfamily along an elevation gradient, not a global latitudinal test.
    - Supports: H3, the cold filter. Non-associated species should increase and obligate mutualists decrease with low MAT, low minimum temperatures and short seasons.
    - PDF: paywalled.

16. **Engelhardt EK, Bowler DE, Dolek M, Opolka MK, Hof C (2025; online 2024).** Myrmecophily is not a risk factor for long-term occupancy trends of central European Lycaenidae butterflies. *Insect Conservation and Diversity* 18: 107–115. doi:10.1111/icad.12782
    - Status: VERIFIED. Full text available.
    - Finding:
      - The study fits occupancy-detection models to 40-year trends in Bavaria. Obligate myrmecophiles did not decline more than ant-independent species.
      - Five of seven obligate species increased, while five of eight ant-independent species decreased. Facultative trends were ambiguous, and group differences were not significant.
      - European obligate species use several widespread *Myrmica*, *Formica* and *Lasius* species, which may buffer larvae against environmental pressures.
    - Supports: contradicts the assumption that obligate strategies are inherently fragile under recent climate and land-use change. It is consistent with nest buffering (H3 corollary).
    - PDF: **engelhardt_2024_myrmecophily_trends.pdf**

17. **Ballmer GR, Pratt GF (1991).** Quantification of ant attendance (myrmecophily) of lycaenid larvae. *Journal of Research on the Lepidoptera* 30: 95–112. doi:10.5962/p.266637
    - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
    - Finding: a standardised attendance index for 58 mostly Californian lycaenids, tested with *Formica pilicornis*. Attendance varies continuously among species. Dendritic setae may be as important as the dorsal nectary and tentacle organs in recruiting ants.
    - Supports: myrmecophily in the Nearctic is a graded, mostly facultative trait. This is useful for coding facultative versus non-associated species.
    - PDF: **ballmer_1991_ant_attendance.pdf**

18. **Seufert P, Fiedler K (1996).** The influence of ants on patterns of colonization and establishment within a set of coexisting lycaenid butterflies in a south-east Asian tropical rain forest. *Oecologia* 106: 127–136. doi:10.1007/BF00334414
    - Status: VERIFIED (abstract summary only).
    - Finding: on *Saraca thaipingensis* trees in Malaysia, colonisation and establishment by a guild of lycaenids depend on both specialisation to particular ants and opportunistic use of the ants and plant parts available.
    - Supports: H7 at the local scale. Ant presence and identity structure which lycaenids establish.
    - PDF: paywalled.

19. **Warchola N, Bastianelli C, Schultz CB, Crone EE (2015).** Fire increases ant-tending and survival of the Fender's blue butterfly larvae. *Journal of Insect Conservation* 19: 1063–1073. doi:10.1007/s10841-015-9822-1
    - Status: VERIFIED (abstract summary).
    - Finding: in Oregon prairies, previously burned areas had higher oviposition and overwinter larval survival, without a matching change in host chemistry. Tending was affected by temperature (faster ant recruitment when warmer) and by fire history (longer tending bouts). Tending differences may explain the higher survival.
    - Supports: tending benefit increases with temperature (H10), and fire–ant interactions (H4). This is the only experimental fire–tending link found.
    - PDF: paywalled.

## C. Mechanisms: why associate with ants, and what costs and benefits depend on

20. **Atsatt PR (1981).** Lycaenid butterflies and ants: selection for enemy-free space. *American Naturalist* 118: 638–654. doi:10.1086/283859
    - Status: VERIFIED (abstract summary).
    - Finding: ants provide enemy-free space. Ephemeral, hard-to-find larval foods and unpredictable ant resources select *against* ant specialisation, allowing only opportunistic (facultative) use of ants.
    - Supports: H7 and H2. Obligacy should evolve where ant partners are spatially and temporally predictable (dominant, long-lived colonies); facultative strategies prevail where ants are unpredictable.
    - PDF: paywalled.

21. **Pierce NE (1985).** Lycaenid butterflies and ants: selection for nitrogen-fixing and other protein-rich food plants. *American Naturalist* 125: 888–895. doi:10.1086/284387
    - Status: VERIFIED. No abstract in the indices; the content is well documented in later reviews.
    - Finding: ant-associated lycaenids disproportionately use nitrogen-fixing or protein-rich host plants, interpreted as the cost of producing amino-acid-rich secretions for ants.
    - Supports: H8. Myrmecophilous strategies should be over-represented where legumes and N-fixers dominate, as in Australian *Acacia* and the southern African Fabaceae.
    - PDF: paywalled.

22. **Pierce NE, Kitching RL, Buckley RC, Taylor MFJ, Benbow KF (1987).** The costs and benefits of cooperation between the Australian lycaenid butterfly, *Jalmenus evagoras*, and its attendant ants. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* 21: 237–248. doi:10.1007/BF00292505
    - Status: VERIFIED (abstract summary).
    - Finding: four ant-exclusion experiments showed that predation and parasitism are so intense that *J. evagoras* larvae deprived of ants are unlikely to survive.
    - Supports: obligacy arises where enemy pressure is high and ants are reliably present (H2/H7).
    - PDF: paywalled.

23. **Cottrell CB (1984).** Aphytophagy in butterflies: its relationship to myrmecophily. *Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society* 80: 1–57. doi:10.1111/j.1096-3642.1984.tb02318.x
    - Status: VERIFIED.
    - Finding: a classic review arguing that regular or obligate aphytophagy (carnivory, trophallaxis) in lycaenids is rooted in myrmecophily. Espeland et al. 2023 cite it, with Fiedler 1991 and 1998, for the hypothesis that harsh above-ground conditions (fire, dry seasons) triggered shifts into ant nests.
    - Supports: H4 for obligate parasites. Parasitism derives from mutualism.
    - PDF: paywalled.

24. **Kaminski LA, Freitas AVL, Oliveira PS (2010).** Interaction between mutualisms: ant-tended butterflies exploit enemy-free space provided by ant-treehopper associations. *American Naturalist* 176: 322–334. doi:10.1086/655427
    - Status: VERIFIED (abstract summary).
    - Finding: in the Brazilian cerrado, females of the facultative *Parrhasius polibetes* oviposit preferentially near ant–treehopper associations. Larvae gain enemy-free space there.
    - Supports: H7. Lycaenid myrmecophily depends on the density of ant–hemipteran trophobiosis, so ant availability is mediated by carbohydrate sources.
    - PDF: paywalled.

## D. Phylogenetics and historical biogeography (lineage history; deep-time climate)

25. **Espeland M, Chazot N, Condamine FL, Lemmon AR, Lemmon EM, Pringle E, Heath A, Collins S, Tiren W, Mutiso M, et al., Pierce NE (2023).** Rapid radiation of ant parasitic butterflies during the Miocene aridification of Africa. *Ecology and Evolution* 13: e10046. doi:10.1002/ece3.10046
    - Status: VERIFIED. Full text read.
    - Finding:
      - The Euchrysops section originated with the Miombo woodlands about 22 Mya and spread into drier biomes in the late Miocene. Net diversification of the non-parasitic lineages fell to a minimum around 10 Mya and was negative between 15 and 5 Mya.
      - Phyto-predaceous *Lepidochrysops* diversified rapidly from about 6.5 Mya, when the life history likely evolved.
      - Southern African montane grasslands were the stepping stone into the Fynbos and Karoo. No phytophagous lineage of the clade reached those arid biomes except *Orachrysops*, which feeds underground on rootstocks tended by *Camponotus*.
      - The authors conclude that the results are *consistent with* aridification selecting for nest-dwelling, with nests offering refuge from fire and food when vegetation is scarce.
    - Correction: the fire and drought refuge is not directly tested.
    - Supports: H4 and deep-time aridification. Parasite share should be higher in arid and seasonally arid biomes with a long aridification history, and forest-dwelling *Lepidochrysops* should be absent.
    - PDF: **espeland_2023_lepidochrysops_miocene.pdf**

26. **Kaliszewska ZA, Lohman DJ, Sommer K, Adelson G, Rand DB, Mathew J, Talavera G, Pierce NE (2015).** When caterpillars attack: biogeography and life history evolution of the Miletinae (Lepidoptera: Lycaenidae). *Evolution* 69: 571–588. doi:10.1111/evo.12599
    - Status: VERIFIED (PubMed 25639142).
    - Finding:
      - The wholly aphytophagous Miletinae comprise about 190 species, mainly in Old World tropics and subtropics. They probably diverged from an African ancestor near the start of the Eocene, with four Africa–Asia dispersals.
      - Prey and ant use are phylogenetically conserved: related miletines eat related Hemiptera and associate with related ants.
      - Host-ant-finding adaptations were probably retained from mutualistic ancestors.
    - Supports: H6. Entomophagous (parasite-type) strategies are clade-bound and restricted to Old World warm regions.
    - PDF: paywalled.

27. **Boyle JH, Kaliszewska ZA, Espeland M, Suderman TR, Fleming J, Heath A, Pierce NE (2015; online 2014).** Phylogeny of the Aphnaeinae: myrmecophilous African butterflies with carnivorous and herbivorous life histories. *Systematic Entomology* 40: 169–182. doi:10.1111/syen.12098
    - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
    - Finding: Aphnaeinae comprise 278 species and are largely African. Larvae are typically ant-mutualistic and feed on 23 plant families. At least one species in each of 9 of the 17 genera is aphytophagous. The subfamily has an African origin with a single dispersal to Asia. The ancestor likely associated with *Crematogaster* ants and Fabales plants.
    - Supports: H6. Southern African obligate and parasite richness is inherited from an African, *Crematogaster*-associated clade. Also H8 (Fabales).
    - PDF: paywalled.

28. **Talavera G, Kaliszewska ZA, Heath A, Pierce NE (2020).** Recent diversification of *Chrysoritis* butterflies in the South African Cape (Lepidoptera: Lycaenidae). *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution* 148: 106817. doi:10.1016/j.ympev.2020.106817
    - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
    - Finding: the genus has 43 species and is about 32 My old. West Fynbos was colonised about 9 Mya, followed by long stasis, then rapid Pleistocene diversification of the *thysbe* group around 2 Mya. The authors attribute the burst to species interactions with ants and plants, key innovations or new niche space, not to colonisation of new areas.
    - Supports: the Cape obligate fauna is partly a Pleistocene radiation. Quaternary climate oscillations in a stable, buffered region could have promoted speciation (H5, nuanced).
    - PDF: paywalled (OA status unclear).

29. **Vila R, Bell CD, Macniven R, Goldman-Huertas B, Ree RH, Marshall CR, Bálint Z, Johnson K, Benyamini D, Pierce NE (2011).** Phylogeny and palaeoecology of *Polyommatus* blue butterflies show Beringia was a climate-regulated gateway to the New World. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 278: 2737–2744. doi:10.1098/rspb.2010.2213
    - Status: VERIFIED. Full text available.
    - Finding: Polyommatini reached the New World through Beringia in five invasions over the past 11 My. Climatic conditions in Beringia filtered which taxa crossed. Miocene–Pleistocene cooling left a strong signal, and thermal tolerances are phylogenetically conserved.
    - Supports: H6 and H5. Cold-filtered dispersal explains why New World lycaenid faunas are drawn from cold-tolerant, mostly facultative or non-associated lineages.
    - PDF: **vila_2011_polyommatus_beringia.pdf**

30. **Kawahara AY, Storer C, Carvalho APS, et al. (2023).** A global phylogeny of butterflies reveals their evolutionary history, ancestral hosts and biogeographic origins. *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 7: 903–913. doi:10.1038/s41559-023-02041-9
    - Status: VERIFIED. Abstract read.
    - Finding: 391 genes from about 2,300 species (92% of genera) place the origin of butterflies about 100 Mya in what is now the Americas, with Fabaceae as the likely ancestral host. Butterflies crossed Beringia soon after the Cretaceous Thermal Maximum and diversified in the Palaeotropics. Most species are specialists on one host family.
    - Supports: the phylogenetic backbone for lineage-history controls (H6).
    - PDF: **kawahara_2023_butterfly_phylogeny.pdf**

31. **Schär S, Eastwood R, Arnaldi KG, Talavera G, Kaliszewska ZA, Boyle JH, Espeland M, Nash DR, Vila R, Pierce NE (2018).** Ecological specialization is associated with genetic structure in the ant-associated butterfly family Lycaenidae. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 285: 20181158. doi:10.1098/rspb.2018.1158
    - Status: VERIFIED. Full text available.
    - Finding: mitochondrial sequences from 8,282 specimens of 967 species show that more ecologically specialised lycaenids (ants and plants) have greater genetic divergence, higher haplotype diversity and stronger isolation by distance. Carnivores have higher substitution rates.
    - Supports: H5. Obligate (specialised) species are poorer dispersers, so they should be rarer where post-LGM recolonisation was required (high climate velocity) and enriched where climates were stable.

They spend this "expendable energy" on carbon-rich defences, lignified tissue and readily digestible exudates such as nectar and gums. The resulting low-nutrient biomass fuels intense fire, which worsens nutrient poverty. The theory accounts for nitrogen-fixing plants being major components of most Australian vegetation and for Australia having the highest proportion of myrmecochorous plants of any continent.
    - Supports: H1 (carbohydrate excess, then ant abundance and dominance, then obligate myrmecophily) and H8 (N-fixers). Variables: soil P, fire frequency.
    - PDF: paywalled.

## Hypotheses from this literature

Notation: NA = non-ant-associated, FAC = facultative, OM = obligate mutualist, OP = obligate parasite. Arrows give the predicted direction of each strategy's proportion along the named gradient.

**H1. Nutrient poverty, carbohydrate excess and ant dominance** (Pierce 1987 as summarised by Pierce & Dankowicz 2022; Orians & Milewski 2007; Davidson et al. 2003; Blüthgen et al. 2004; Lengyel et al. 2009; Hopper 2009).
- Mechanism: on P-poor, old, weathered soils, plants produce excess carbohydrate exudates. These support abundant, dominant, trophobiotic ants, which makes lycaenid secretions profitable and ant partners predictable.
- Predictions with increasing soil infertility and exudate supply: OM ↑, OP ↑ (parasites arise from dense mutualisms; Fiedler 2012), FAC ↓ (relative), NA ↓.
- Variables: soil P and total nutrients (SoilGrids), soil age and weathering, myrmecochore and extrafloral-nectary plant density.
- Climate enters indirectly: aridity and fire frequency correlate with infertility, so control for them.

**H2. Hot, open habitats with dominant ants** (Andersen 1995; Fiedler 2001, 2021; Eastwood & Fraser 1999; Parr 2008; Atsatt 1981; Schultheiss et al. 2022).
- Mechanism: obligate associations need ecologically dominant, large, long-lived, spatially predictable colonies (*Iridomyrmex*, *Anonychomyrma*, *Crematogaster*, *Oecophylla*, *Anoplolepis*, *Camponotus*). These peak in hot, open, seasonally dry habitats (savanna, woodland, shrubland).
- Predictions with temperature, insolation and openness: OM ↑, OP ↑, FAC roughly flat to ↓, NA ↓.
- Variables: MAT, maximum temperature of the warmest month, solar radiation, tree cover (inverse), precipitation seasonality.

**H3. Cold-climate filter** (Pellissier et al. 2012; Pierce & Dankowicz 2022; Bishop et al. 2016; Kaspari & Vargo 1995; Thomas et al. 1998).
- Mechanism: low temperatures and short seasons reduce ant foraging and partner reliability and lengthen larval development. Strong myrmecophily is filtered out or lost in cold climates.
- Predictions with decreasing temperature (latitude and elevation): NA ↑, FAC ↓ at the coldest extreme but dominant at mid-latitudes, OM ↓.
- OP: two-sided. Nest-dwelling parasites (*Phengaris*) are buffered inside nests (Fiedler 2006), so OP should decline less than OM and may persist in cool-temperate Palaearctic grasslands with *Myrmica*.
- Variables: MAT, minimum temperature of the coldest month, growing degree days, growing-season length, temperature seasonality, elevation.

**H4. Harsh-surface refuge: aridity, fire and dry seasons** (Cottrell 1984; Fiedler 1991, 1998, as cited by Espeland et al. 2023; Espeland et al. 2023; Pierce & Dankowicz 2022; Warchola et al. 2015).
- Mechanism: in seasonally arid, fire-prone biomes, larvae that move into ant nests escape fire, desiccation and food scarcity. This selects for nest-dwelling and parasitic life histories.
- Predictions with aridity, dry-season length and fire frequency: OP ↑↑, OM ↑ (especially nest-associated, underground-feeding mutualists such as *Orachrysops*), FAC ↓, NA ↓. Forest cells should show fewer OP.
- Variables: aridity index (P/PET), precipitation seasonality, dry-season length, fire return interval or burned-area fraction (MODIS), C4 grass cover.
- Deep time: the timing of regional aridification (Miocene–Pliocene; for example, the age of the savanna and grassland biomes).

**H5. Long-term climatic stability versus Quaternary instability** (Dunn et al. 2009; Hopper 2009; Pierce & Dankowicz 2022; Schär et al. 2018; Vila et al. 2011; Thomas 1993).
- Mechanism: obligate specialists are dispersal-limited (strong genetic structure) and depend on co-occurring hosts and ants. They should accumulate where climates were stable and be filtered out where glacial cycles forced range shifts. The Northern Hemisphere suffered more climatic upheaval and extinction (Dunn et al. 2009) and was recolonised by generalists.
- Predictions with LGM temperature anomaly, post-LGM climate velocity and Quaternary instability: OM ↓, OP ↓, FAC ↑, NA ↑.
- Caveat (Andersen 2016; Talavera et al. 2020): in Australia and the Cape, glacial *aridity* (not cold) fragmented ranges and may have *promoted* speciation, including the Pleistocene burst in *Chrysoritis*. LGM precipitation anomalies could therefore correlate *positively* with obligate richness in the Southern Hemisphere.
- Test LGM ΔT and ΔP separately, and test their interaction with hemisphere.

**H6. Lineage history and niche conservatism** (Pierce et al. 2002; Pierce & Dankowicz 2022; Fiedler 1998a, 2021; Kaliszewska et al. 2015; Boyle et al. 2015; Als et al. 2004; Kawahara et al. 2023; Espeland et al. 2015; Vila et al. 2011).
- Mechanism: obligate-rich clades (Aphnaeinae, Miletinae, *Lepidochrysops*, Australian Theclinae and Polyommatini) are confined to the Old World tropics and southern regions. Only facultative or non-associated lineages (Lycaeninae, Theclini, Eumaeini, Polyommatini, the Spalgis–Feniseca clade) crossed Beringia or the Atlantic into the Holarctic and the New World. Neotropical myrmecophily lives largely in Riodinidae.
- Predictions: strategy proportions are explained largely by biogeographic realm and tribal composition, and climate effects shrink once clade composition is accounted for. Expect a strong phylogenetic signal and realm fixed effects.
- Tests: phylogenetic variance partitioning, analyses within tribes, and climate effects estimated within realms.

**H7. Partner-ant availability and prevalence** (Fiedler 2001, 2012, 2021; Eastwood & Fraser 1999; DeVries 1991; Kaminski et al. 2010; Tartally et al. 2019; Glasier et al. 2018; Styrsky & Eubanks 2007).
- Mechanism: facultative associations track the ecological prevalence of trophobiont-tending ants. Obligate associations need specific dominant hosts, and parasites arise in ant genera that already carry many mutualists.
- Predictions: FAC ↑ with total abundance of tending ants. OM ↑ with the presence and abundance of dominant partner genera. OP ↑ with *Camponotus*, *Crematogaster* and *Myrmica* presence and with the local OM share.
- Neotropics: dominance by leafcutter and army ants, which never tend trophobionts, should lower OM and OP (Fiedler 2021).
- Variables: GABI/antmaps genus ranges, the Kass et al. 2022 richness surface, the Schultheiss et al. 2022 abundance map, and the share of Attini and Dorylinae.

**H8. Nitrogen-rich host plants** (Pierce 1985; Fiedler 1995, 1996; Boyle et al. 2015; Orians & Milewski 2007).
- Mechanism: protein-rich food (legumes and other N-fixers) lets larvae produce nutritious secretions.
- Predictions with legume and N-fixer dominance: FAC ↑, OM ↑, NA ↓. OP is neutral, since parasites eat ants.
- Variables: Fabaceae share of the regional flora, N-fixer cover. These are confounded with aridity in Australia and Africa.

**H9. Social-parasite latitude and host-colony traits** (Gray & Rabeling 2023; Buschinger 2009; Borowiec et al. 2021; Kaspari & Vargo 1995).
- Mechanism: ant-on-ant social parasites peak in the cool-temperate Northern Hemisphere, associated with polygyny, dependent colony founding and dense host populations.
- If the same host-side mechanism applied to lycaenid parasites: OP ↑ with Northern-Hemisphere latitude (*Phengaris*/*Myrmica*) and OP independent of aridity.
- The observed concentration of lycaenid OP in warm, arid southern Africa contradicts this, which suggests H4 and H6 dominate for lycaenids.
- Variables: latitude, MAT, host-genus colony traits (GlobalAnts).

**H10. Thermal dependence of tending** (Warchola et al. 2015; Kaspari et al. 2000; Barton & Ives 2014; Diamond et al. 2012; Cerdá et al. 1997).
- Mechanism: ant recruitment and activity rise with temperature and NPP, which makes tending more reliable in warm climates. Extreme heat and high temperature variability can disrupt dominance hierarchies and mutualisms.
- Predictions with temperature: FAC and OM ↑ up to a warm optimum, then plateau or decline in the hottest, most variable climates. NA is the mirror image.
- Variables: MAT, maximum temperature of the warmest month, diurnal and seasonal temperature range, NPP.

**H11. Sodium and phosphorus limitation of ants** (Kaspari et al. 2008; Kaspari 2020; Kaspari et al. 2020; Pierce & Dankowicz 2022).
- Mechanism: where Na or P is scarce, ants value nutrient-supplying partners more, so caterpillars can secure stronger, obligate associations.
- Predictions with distance inland, low plant Na and low soil P: OM ↑ and OP ↑ (parasites exploit strong ant attraction).
- Variables: distance to coast, modelled atmospheric Na deposition, soil P.

## Contradictions and gaps

- **Lineage versus environment.** Pierce et al. (2002) attribute the southern excess of obligate species partly to lineage composition, while Pierce & Dankowicz (2022) list climate, soils, aridification and glaciation as non-exclusive drivers. No study has partitioned these at a global scale, which is the gap the new study fills.
  - The equal-temperate-climate contrast between the Western Palaearctic (few obligate species) and temperate Australia (many) argues against climate alone. It rests on Fiedler 1998b, which is unverified.
- **Ant social parasites versus lycaenid ant parasites.** Ant social parasites show an inverse latitudinal gradient concentrated in the cool Northern Hemisphere (Gray & Rabeling 2023). Lycaenid ant parasites concentrate in warm, arid southern Africa and Australia; only *Phengaris* fits the Northern pattern.
  - *Formica*, the richest social-parasite genus, has no lycaenid parasites (Fiedler 2012).
  - "Parasitism of ants is a cool-climate phenomenon" therefore does not transfer to Lepidoptera.
- **Cold climates and ant availability.** Kaspari & Vargo (1995) and Kaspari et al. (2000) show larger colonies and higher nest densities in seasonal, cold-winter sites, which seemingly favours partner reliability. This conflicts with a simple "few good partners in cold climates" view (Pellissier et al. 2012). The cold filter may act on larval development and host plants rather than on ant availability.
- **Ant richness and abundance versus obligate share.**
  - Ant richness is higher in the Southern Hemisphere (Dunn et al. 2009) and in arid Australia (Andersen 2016), which fits the obligate pattern.
  - Ant abundance peaks in tropical forests and savannas, and the Neotropics are rich in ants yet poor in obligate lycaenids. Ant quantity is not enough; ant community *composition* matters (dominant trophobiont-tenders versus leafcutter and army ants; Fiedler 2021).
- **Dominant ants are not universally good partners.** Australian facultative species associate with dominant ants less often than expected (Eastwood & Fraser 1999), and *Formica obscuripes* can act as a parasite of the *Glaucopsyche*–ant association (Fraser et al. 2001). This conflicts with dominance as the general driver; it applies mainly to obligate species.
- **Sign of the past-climate effect.**
  - Stability hypotheses (Dunn et al. 2009; Hopper 2009; Schär et al. 2018) predict more obligate species where climate was stable.
  - Andersen (2016) and Talavera et al. (2020) suggest glacial aridity and Pleistocene oscillations *promoted* speciation in Southern-Hemisphere ants and *Chrysoritis*.
  - Espeland et al. (2023) attribute the *Lepidochrysops* radiation to directional Miocene aridification, not to stability.
  - Separate LGM temperature from precipitation anomalies, and separate directional deep-time aridification from Quaternary oscillation.
- **Fire refuge has not been tested directly.** It rests on consistency arguments (Espeland et al. 2023). The only field data (Warchola et al. 2015) show prescribed fire killing larvae in the short term, while increasing ant tending and survival afterwards.
- **Vulnerability of obligate species.** Engelhardt et al. (2025) found no greater decline in obligate central-European lycaenids over 40 years, while Pierce & Dankowicz (2022) note that over 60% of threatened Red-List lycaenids and riodinids are obligate. Fragility may be regional or habitat-specific rather than climatic.
- **Thomas et al. 2009 is about grazing and turf structure, not warming.** The climate link for *Maculinea* comes from Thomas et al. 1998 and Thomas 1993 (microclimate of the host ant). No published study tests a climate effect on *Maculinea* distributions at the continental scale; a published host-use synthesis exists (Tartally et al. 2019) but does not test climate.
- **Data gaps.**
  - Neotropical lycaenid life histories are poorly documented (Pierce et al. 2002; Fiedler 2021). Riodinids, with about 20% ant-associated, carry much Neotropical myrmecophily and are outside a lycaenid-only dataset.
  - Tropical African and Southeast Asian obligate associations are under-documented (Pierce & Dankowicz 2022).
  - Missingness is geographically non-random and could bias strategy proportions.
- **No global ant-dominance or trophobiosis map exists.** GlobalAnts traits (Parr et al. 2017) plus GABI genus ranges could approximate one. Global soil Na and P layers are coarse. Within-species elevation or latitude studies of attendance intensity are almost absent: only Pellissier et al. (2012), at the species level, plus temperature effects on recruitment (Warchola et al. 2015).
- **Myrmecochory is not a clean proxy for nutrient poverty.** It is also concentrated in Holarctic temperate deciduous-forest herbs (Lengyel et al. 2009).
